<section id="working-papers">
  <h2>Working papers</h2>

  <div class="paper">
    <div class="paper-title">
      <a class="paper-title-link"
        href="/docs/working_papers/firm_upgrading_trade_migration.pdf"
        target="_blank" rel="noopener">
        Immigration and Firm-level Upgrading as Exports Boosters in Developing Countries
      </a>
    </div>
    <div class="paper-meta">
      with <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://www.leonardopenalozapacheco.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
        Leonardo Peñaloza-Pacheco
      </a>
    </div>
    <details class="abstract">
      <summary>Abstract</summary>
      <p>We study how a labor supply shock affects firm-level upgrading and exports in Colombian manufacturing: the Venezuelan exodus raised labor supply across all skill groups but relatively more among the high-skilled. Combining confidential firm-level data on production, innovation, and exports with a shift-share instrument based on distance to Venezuela, we find that more exposed firms increased R&amp;D hiring and spending, used higher-quality imported inputs, improved production and organizational processes, switched toward skill- and R&amp;D-intensive products, and became more likely to obtain quality certifications. This upgrading translated into stronger export performance, particularly of differentiated goods to new and richer destinations. A multi-country trade model rationalizes these patterns through two quality margins: the migration-induced shift in relative skill supply lowers the relative cost of R&amp;D tasks, raises firms’ R&amp;D intensity and product quality, and selects upgraded firms into more demanding markets.</p>
    </details>

  <h2>Publications</h2>

  <div class="paper">
    <div class="paper-title">
      <a class="paper-title-link" href="/docs/publications/migration_income_distribution.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
        From displacement to integration: mitigating the distributional effect of immigration through an amnesty program
      </a>
    </div>
    <div class="paper-meta">
      with <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://jmartinezcorrea.github.io/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Julian Martinez-Correa</a>, <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://www.leonardopenalozapacheco.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Leonardo Peñaloza-Pacheco</a>, and
      <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://www.cedlas.econo.unlp.edu.ar/wp/en/lgasparini/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Leonardo Gasparini</a>
    </div>
    <div class="paper-cite">
      <span class="journal">Journal of Economic Geography</span>, 2025, 25(6), 809–830
    </div>
    <details class="abstract">
      <summary>Abstract</summary>
      <p>We examine how the massive exodus of Venezuelans and an amnesty program aimed at their integration affected income distribution in Colombia. Using RIF regressions in an instrumental variables approach, we find that the exodus had a negative effect on the lower tail of native Colombians’ income distribution, while the amnesty program partially mitigated this impact. Our findings posit downgrading as the driving mechanism. Employment restrictions forced Venezuelan migrants into lower-paying, more routinized jobs despite their qualifications, exacerbating pressures on the low-skilled labor segment in Colombia. The regularization program mitigated the extent of downgrading, thereby alleviating the unequalizing impact of migration.</p>
    </details>

<section id="predoc">
  <h2>Pre-doctoral publications</h2>

  <div class="paper">
    <div class="paper-title">
      <a class="paper-title-link" href="/docs/publications/min_wage_inequality.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
        Does the Minimum Wage Affect Wage Inequality? A Study for the Six Largest Latin American Economies
      </a>
    </div>
    <div class="paper-meta">
      with
      <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://www.cedlas.econo.unlp.edu.ar/wp/en/lramirez/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Lucía Ramírez Leira</a>
      and
      <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://www.cedlas.econo.unlp.edu.ar/wp/en/lgasparini/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Leonardo Gasparini</a>
    </div>
    <div class="paper-cite">
      <span class="journal">The Journal of Development Studies</span>, 2024, 60(4), 494–510
    </div>
    <details class="abstract">
      <summary>Abstract</summary>
      <p>Minimum wage (MW) policies are widespread in the developing world and yet their effects are still unclear. In this paper we explore the effect of national MW policies in Latin America’s six largest economies by exploiting the heterogeneity in the bite of the national minimum wage across local labor markets and over time. We find evidence that the MW has a compression effect on the wage distribution of formal workers. The effect was particularly large during the 2000s, a decade of sustained growth and strong labor markets. In contrast, the effect seems to vanish in the 2010s, a decade of much weaker labor markets. We also find suggestive evidence of a lighthouse effect: the MW seems to have an equalizing effect also on the wage distribution of informal workers.</p>
    </details>
  </div>

  <div class="paper">
    <div class="paper-title">
      <a class="paper-title-link" href="/docs/publications/routinization_employment.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
        Routinization and Employment: Evidence for Latin America
      </a>
    </div>
    <div class="paper-meta">
      with
      <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://sites.google.com/site/irenebrambilla/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Irene Brambilla</a>,
      <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://www.andres-cesar.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Andrés César</a>,
      <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://www.guillermofalcone.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Guillermo Falcone</a>
      and
      <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://www.cedlas.econo.unlp.edu.ar/wp/en/lgasparini/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Leonardo Gasparini</a>
    </div>
    <div class="paper-cite">
      <span class="journal">Revista Desarrollo y Sociedad</span>, 2023, (95), 131–176
    </div>
    <details class="abstract">
      <summary>Abstract</summary>
      <p>We study changes in employment across occupations characterized by different degrees of exposure to routinization in the six largest Latin American economies over the past two decades. We combine our own indicators of routine task content (RTC), based on information from the Programme for the International Assessment of Adult Competencies (PIAAC), with labor market microdata from harmonized national household surveys. We find that growth in employment was inversely related to the automatability of tasks typically performed in each occupation, and positively correlated with the initial wage. Consequently, the share of high RTC occupations in total employment decreased in all countries and periods. This decline is linked to shifts in the economic structure towards sectors more intensive in low RTC occupations, as well as changes in the intensity of use of different occupations within sectors.</p>
    </details>
  </div>

  <div class="paper">
    <div class="paper-title">
      <a class="paper-title-link" href="/docs/publications/asymmetric_risks_automation.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener">
        The Asymmetric Risks of Automation in Latin America
      </a>
    </div>
    <div class="paper-meta">
      with
      <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://sites.google.com/site/irenebrambilla/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Irene Brambilla</a>,
      <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://www.andres-cesar.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Andrés César</a>,
      <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://www.guillermofalcone.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Guillermo Falcone</a>
      and
      <a class="coauthor-link" href="https://www.cedlas.econo.unlp.edu.ar/wp/en/lgasparini/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Leonardo Gasparini</a>
    </div>
    <div class="paper-cite">
      <span class="journal">Desarrollo Económico</span>, 2022, 62(235), 234–253
    </div>
    <details class="abstract">
      <summary>Abstract</summary>
      <p>In this paper we characterize workers’ risks from automation in the near future in the six largest Latin American economies as a function of the exposure to routinization of the tasks that they perform and the potential automation of their occupation. We combine (i) indicators of potential automatability by occupation and (ii) workers’ information on occupation and other labor and demographic variables. We find that the ongoing process of automation is likely to significantly affect the structure of employment. In particular, unskilled and semi-skilled workers are more at risk of bearing a disproportionate share of the adjustment costs. Automation will probably be a more dangerous threat for equality than for overall employment.</p>
    </details>
